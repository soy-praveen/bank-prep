## Answer Key, Solutions & Tricks

### Error Spotting

**Q1. Answer: (c) have been -> "has been".**
Rule broken: Subject-verb agreement with "one of the...". The true subject is the singular "One", not the plural "students". "One... has been suspended."
**Trick:** *"One of the" always takes a singular verb.* The plural noun after "of" is only describing the group; the head is "One". Mentally cross out the "of the students" phrase and the singular subject stands alone.

**Q2. Answer: (c) have crossed -> "has crossed".**
Rule broken: Subject-verb agreement. "The number of..." is singular and takes a singular verb. (Contrast: "A number of..." is plural.)
**Trick:** *"The number of" -> singular; "A number of" -> plural.* Ignore the "of applicants" phrase — the subject is "The number".

**Q3. Answer: (c) for the year 2015 -> "since 2015".**
Rule broken: since vs for. 2015 is a point in time, so it takes "since". "For" is used only for a duration/period.
**Trick:** *since = a point in time (since 2015, since Monday); for = a length of time (for two years, for a decade).* With the present perfect continuous ("has been living"), a specific starting point needs "since".

**Q4. Answer: (b) more senior than me -> "senior to me".**
Rule broken: Latin comparatives. Words like senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, prefer take "to", never "than", and are not used with "more".
**Trick:** *senior / superior / junior / inferior / prior / prefer + "to" (never "than", never "more").*

**Q5. Answer: (c) were praised -> "was praised".**
Rule broken: Subject-verb agreement with "along with". Phrases like along with, together with, as well as, in addition to do not change the number of the subject. The subject is singular "The manager".
**Trick:** *Ignore the "along with / as well as / together with" phrase (usually set off by commas) — match the verb to the real subject before it.* Manager = singular = "was".

**Q6. Answer: (a) fewer -> "less".**
Rule broken: less vs fewer. Sugar is an uncountable noun, so it takes "less". "Fewer" is only for countable plural nouns.
**Trick:** *fewer = things you can count (fewer chairs, fewer errors); less = things you cannot count / measure (less sugar, less water, less time).*

**Q7. Answer: (b) when -> "than".**
Rule broken: Correlative pairing. "No sooner" is always followed by "than", not "when". (Note also the correct inversion: "No sooner had the bell rung...".)
**Trick:** *no sooner ... than; hardly/scarcely ... when; such ... that.* Memorise the fixed partner of each opener.

**Q8. Answer: (b) I think -> redundancy.**
Rule broken: Redundancy. "In my opinion" and "I think" express the same idea; using both is repetitive. Keep one only — "In my opinion, the plan is far too risky."
**Trick:** *Watch for double-expression redundancy: "In my opinion, I think", "return back", "reason is because", "each and every one". If two parts say the same thing, one is the error.*

### Sentence Improvement

**Q9. Answer: (c) when the lights went off (No improvement).**
Rule broken: none — the pairing is already correct. "Hardly/Scarcely" is completed by "when", not "than" or "then".
**Trick:** *hardly/scarcely ... when* (partner check again). Do not confuse it with *no sooner ... than*.

**Q10. Answer: (e) No improvement — "were divided".**
Rule broken: none. A collective noun like "committee" takes a plural verb when its members act as individuals (here, "divided in their opinions" shows disagreement among members). "Were divided" is correct.
**Trick:** *A collective noun is plural when the group is split/acting individually ("the jury were divided"), singular when it acts as one unit ("the jury has reached a verdict").*

**Q11. Answer: (b) to tea.**
Rule broken: "prefer" takes "to", never "than".
**Trick:** *prefer + noun + "to" + noun.* (Same Latin-comparative family as senior/superior.)

**Q12. Answer: (a) had known.**
Rule broken: Third conditional. The "if" clause of an unreal past condition uses the past perfect ("had known"), and the main clause uses "would have + participle". "Would have" never appears in the if-clause.
**Trick:** *If + had + past participle, ... would have + past participle. Never put "would" in the "if" half.*

**Q13. Answer: (c) how.**
Rule broken: Double conjunction / redundancy. "That" and "how" both introduce the clause; using both is wrong. Keep "how" alone: "explained how the vaccine works".
**Trick:** *Do not stack conjunctions: "that how", "but yet", "because... so", "although... but" are all errors. Keep one connector.*

**Q14. Answer: (b) were aware.**
Rule broken: neither...nor proximity rule. With "neither...nor" (and "either...or"), the verb agrees with the nearer subject. The nearer subject is "the students" (plural), so "were aware".
**Trick:** *neither/nor & either/or: the verb matches the subject CLOSEST to it. Reorder mentally so the closest noun sets the verb.*

### Para-Jumbles

**Set 1. Correct order: B - D - A - C - E.**

- **B** opens: it introduces the full subject ("millions of people") and the topic (resolutions).
- **D** follows: "These goals" points back to the resolutions in B.
- **A** brings the contrast: "However, most of these plans collapse".
- **C** explains the collapse: "The reason is... a lack of a realistic system".
- **E** concludes: "Experts therefore advise..." gives the takeaway.

**Q15. Answer: (b) B.** B is the opener — it names the full subject with no back-reference; every other sentence depends on something already introduced (These goals, these plans, The reason, therefore).

**Q16. Answer: (e) E.** "Experts therefore advise..." is the concluding recommendation; "therefore" signals a wrap-up.

**Trick:** *The opener contains the full noun (here "millions of people"), never a pronoun or a back-pointing word. Pronouns and demonstratives (These goals, these plans, The reason) must hug the noun they refer to, so they cannot start the paragraph. Signal words — however, therefore, as a result — mark the middle or the end, never the start.*

**Set 2. Correct order: B - D - C - A.**

- **B** opens: it introduces the actors ("A small cooperative") and the action (buying a shared pump).
- **D** gives the earlier situation: "Before this, each family had struggled alone" — contrasting the past with B.
- **C** states the effect of the pump: "The pump allowed them to grow a second crop".
- **A** gives the final result: "As a result, farmers... were able to double their income".

**Q17. Answer: (d) D.** After the opener B, sentence D ("Before this...") sets the earlier backdrop before the consequences unfold.

**Q18. Answer: (a) B-D-C-A.**

**Trick:** *"As a result" and "Before this" are position markers. "As a result" (A) almost always lands last — it is an outcome. "Before this" (D) must sit right after the event it contrasts. "The pump" in C hugs the pump introduced in B, confirming C follows the pump's purchase.*

### Word Swap / Word Usage

**Q19. Answer: (b) flavour - salt.**
Rule: The two words sit in each other's slots. Interchanging "flavour" and "salt" gives the logical sentence: "The chef added a pinch of **salt** to enhance the **flavour** of the soup." You add salt (a physical pinch) in order to enhance flavour, not the reverse.
**Trick:** *Word Swap: spot the two words that are in each other's place, swap them, and re-read. The version that makes logical sense is right. Check the objects: a "pinch of salt" is physical; "flavour" is the quality being enhanced — only that pairing fits.*

**Q20. Answer: (a) raise - replace.**
Rule: Swapping "raise" and "replace" makes the sentence logical: "The company decided to **replace** its old machines and **raise** the wages of its workers." You replace old machines and raise wages.
**Trick:** *After swapping, check that each verb fits its object: machines are replaced, wages are raised. The pair whose interchange makes BOTH halves sensible is the answer.*

**Q21. Answer: (b) The principal amount of the loan must be repaid within five years.**
Rule: principal vs principle. "Principal" = chief/main, or the original loan sum, or a head of school (noun). "Principle" = a rule or moral truth. Options (a), (c), (d), (e) all need "principle(s)".
**Trick:** *principLE = a ruLE (both end in -le); principAL = the mAin one / the pAL who is the head. The loan's "principal" is the main sum.*

**Q22. Answer: (b) A good wine can complement a rich meal perfectly.**
Rule: complement vs compliment. "Complement" = to complete or go well with something. "Compliment" = praise. All the other options mean praise and need "compliment(s)".
**Trick:** *complEment = complEte (goes together); complIment = "I" like to praise. If it means "goes well with", use complement.*

**Q23. Answer: (c) Poor sleep can seriously affect your concentration.**
Rule: affect vs effect. "Affect" is normally the verb (to influence); "effect" is normally the noun (the result). Options (a), (b), (d), (e) need the noun "effect".
**Trick:** *A = Action = Affect (verb); E = End result = Effect (noun). "Affect" DOES something; an "effect" IS the result.*

**Q24. Answer: (c) An eminent scientist delivered the keynote address.**
Rule: eminent vs imminent. "Eminent" = distinguished/famous. "Imminent" = about to happen. Options (a), (b), (d), (e) describe something about to happen and need "imminent".
**Trick:** *Eminent = Excellent/Esteemed person; Imminent = "in a minute" (about to happen). A famous person is eminent; an approaching storm is imminent.*
