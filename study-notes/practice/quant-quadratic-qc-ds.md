## Answer Key, Solutions & Tricks

### Part A — Quadratic Equations

**Q1. Answer: (d) x <= y**
x^2 - 7x + 12 = 0 gives (x-3)(x-4) = 0, so x = 3 or 4.
y^2 - 9y + 20 = 0 gives (y-4)(y-5) = 0, so y = 4 or 5.
x lies in {3, 4}; y lies in {4, 5}. The largest x (4) equals the smallest y (4), and otherwise x is smaller. So every x is less than or equal to every y: x <= y.
**Trick:** Factor by "product = c, sum = b, then flip the signs." For x^2 - 7x + 12: find two numbers with product +12 and sum -7 (they are -3 and -4), so roots are +3 and +4. To compare, place both root-sets on a number line. When the ranges just touch at one endpoint (4 = 4), the answer carries the "equal to" sign (<= or >=), never the strict one.

**Q2. Answer: (a) x > y**
(x-5)(x-6) = 0, so x = 5 or 6. (y-1)(y-2) = 0, so y = 1 or 2.
The smallest x (5) is bigger than the largest y (2), so x > y for every pairing.
**Trick:** You do not need to test all four pairings. Compare the smallest x with the largest y: if smallest x > largest y, the answer is a clean x > y. If the two ranges do not overlap at all, you always get a strict relation.

**Q3. Answer: (b) x < y**
(x-1)(x-3) = 0, so x = 1 or 3. (y-4)(y-7) = 0, so y = 4 or 7.
Largest x (3) is still below smallest y (4), so x < y.
**Trick:** Mirror of Q2 — largest x < smallest y means a clean x < y.

**Q4. Answer: (e) no relation**
x^2 - x - 6 = 0: product -6, sum -1, numbers are -3 and +2; roots x = 3 or -2.
y^2 - y - 2 = 0: product -2, sum -1, numbers are -2 and +1; roots y = 2 or -1.
x in {-2, 3}, y in {-1, 2}. Test the extremes: x = -2 vs y = -1 gives x < y, but x = 3 vs y = 2 gives x > y. Both directions occur, so no fixed relation.
**Trick:** When a quadratic has a negative constant term (here -6, -2), its roots have opposite signs. Overlapping ranges (the sets interleave) is the signature of answer (e). If you ever find one pairing with x > y and another with x < y, stop immediately and mark "no relation."

**Q5. Answer: (c) x >= y**
(x-4)(x-5) = 0, so x = 4 or 5. (y-3)(y-4) = 0, so y = 3 or 4.
Smallest x (4) equals largest y (4); otherwise x is larger. So x >= y.
**Trick:** Same touching-endpoint rule as Q1, but the direction is reversed, giving >=.

**Q6. Answer: (a) x > y**
3x^2 - 13x + 12 = 0: multiply a*c = 36, need two numbers with product 36 and sum -13, namely -9 and -4. Split: 3x^2 - 9x - 4x + 12 = 3x(x-3) - 4(x-3) = (3x-4)(x-3) = 0, so x = 4/3 (about 1.33) or 3.
2y^2 - 3y + 1 = 0 = (2y-1)(y-1) = 0, so y = 1/2 or 1.
Smallest x (1.33) > largest y (1), so x > y.
**Trick:** For a leading coefficient other than 1, use the a*c split: find factors of (a*c) that add to b, break the middle term, and group. Convert fractions to decimals only for the final range comparison.

**Q7. Answer: (b) x < y**
(x+2)(x+3) = 0, so x = -2 or -3. y^2 - 2y - 3 = 0 = (y-3)(y+1) = 0, so y = 3 or -1.
x in {-3, -2}, y in {-1, 3}. Largest x (-2) is below smallest y (-1), so x < y.
**Trick:** x^2 + 5x + 6 has product +6, sum +5 (both +2, +3), and because the sum is positive with a positive product, both roots are negative (flip +2, +3 to -2, -3). Watch signs: -3 is less than -2, so the "largest x" is -2, not -3.

**Q8. Answer: (e) no relation**
x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0 = (x-4)(x+2) = 0, so x = 4 or -2.
y^2 - 6y + 8 = 0 = (y-2)(y-4) = 0, so y = 2 or 4.
x in {-2, 4}, y in {2, 4}. x = -2 vs y = 2 gives x < y, but x = 4 vs y = 2 gives x > y (and x = 4 = y = 4). Mixed directions, so no relation.
**Trick:** A shared root (4 appears in both) plus an outlier that breaks the other way is a classic "no relation" trap. The presence of one equal pairing does not make the answer (e) equality — you must check whether the other pairings agree.

### Part B — Quantity Comparison

Method for this whole part: compute each quantity fully and compare the two numbers. Unlike Quadratics, there is nothing to "read off a line" — just evaluate Q1, evaluate Q2, then pick the sign.

**Q9. Answer: (b) Q1 < Q2**
Q1 = 15% of 240 = 0.15 * 240 = 36. Q2 = 25% of 160 = 0.25 * 160 = 40. 36 < 40.
**Trick:** For percentages, use the "of-swap": 15% of 240 equals 240% of 15 = 36; likewise 25% of 160 = 40. Swapping to the friendlier base often lets you compute in your head.

**Q10. Answer: (a) Q1 > Q2**
Q1 = (24 + 36 + 48)/3 = 108/3 = 36. Q2 = (30 + 40 + 20)/3 = 90/3 = 30. 36 > 30.
**Trick:** Compare the sums, not the averages — same divisor (3), so 108 vs 90 decides it instantly.

**Q11. Answer: (e) Q1 = Q2**
Q1 = 200 * 1.20 = 240. Q2 = 300 * 0.80 = 240. Equal.
**Trick:** Profit multiplies cost by (1 + rate); loss multiplies by (1 - rate). Compute both selling prices and compare. Do not assume a bigger cost price wins — a loss can pull it down to the same value.

**Q12. Answer: (a) Q1 > Q2**
Q1: A : B = 3 : 4, total 7 parts = 42, so 1 part = 6, and B = 4 * 6 = 24.
Q2: C : D = 5 : 2, C = 30 means 1 part = 6, so D = 2 * 6 = 12. 24 > 12.
**Trick:** Ratios need one real value to unlock the unit. Find "one part," then scale to the quantity asked. Here both ratios happen to have unit 6, but that is a coincidence — always recompute.

**Q13. Answer: (b) Q1 < Q2**
Combined one-day work = 1/12 + 1/6 = 1/12 + 2/12 = 3/12 = 1/4, so together they finish in 4 days. 4 < 5.
**Trick:** Time together = product / sum of individual times = (12 * 6)/(12 + 6) = 72/18 = 4 days. This shortcut works for exactly two workers.

**Q14. Answer: (a) Q1 > Q2**
Q1 (CI) = 1000 * (1.1)^2 - 1000 = 1210 - 1000 = 210. Q2 (SI) = (1000 * 10 * 2)/100 = 200. 210 > 200.
**Trick:** For 2 years, CI - SI = P * (rate)^2 = 1000 * (0.1)^2 = 10. Compound interest always exceeds simple interest (for more than one year), so Q1 > Q2 without full computation — the exact gap is just 10.

**Q15. Answer: (d) Q1 <= Q2**
x^2 - 7x + 12 = 0 gives x = 3 or 4, so Q1 is 3 or 4. Q2 = 4. Since Q1 can be 3 (less than 4) or 4 (equal to 4), it is never greater; Q1 <= Q2.
**Trick:** When one quantity is a range that touches the other at its top end, the answer takes the "or equal" sign (<=). This is the QC analogue of the touching-endpoint rule from Part A.

### Part C — Data Sufficiency

Method for this whole part: check Statement I ALONE (cover II), then Statement II ALONE (cover I), and only if neither settles it, combine them. Crucially, you never need the final numeric answer — you only judge whether a unique answer WOULD exist. Reasoning "I could solve this" is enough; do not waste time solving.

**Q16. Answer: (d) Both statements together are needed**
I alone: father is 30 years older than the son, but the son's age is unknown, so the father's age is not fixed. Not sufficient.
II alone: son is 12, but nothing links it to the father. Not sufficient.
Together: father = 12 + 30 = 42. Sufficient only when combined.
**Trick:** Two statements that each supply a different missing piece (one gives a relationship, the other a value) almost always point to (d). Note you can see 42 exists without needing to write it — recognizing solvability is the whole job.

**Q17. Answer: (e) Even both together are not sufficient**
I alone: digit sum 9 has many numbers (18, 27, ...). Not sufficient.
II alone: digit difference 3 has many numbers (14, 25, ...). Not sufficient.
Together: digits summing to 9 with a difference of 3 are 6 and 3, but the number could be 63 or 36. Two valid answers means no unique value.
**Trick:** In DS, "two possible answers" counts as NOT sufficient. Even after combining, if order or sign leaves more than one candidate, the answer is (e). Always ask "is the answer unique?" not merely "did I find an answer?"

**Q18. Answer: (c) Either statement alone is sufficient**
I alone: x is a positive integer with x^2 = 49, so x = 7 (the negative root is ruled out by "positive"). Sufficient.
II alone: x = 7 is given directly. Sufficient.
Since each works on its own, the answer is (c).
**Trick:** Watch for constraints like "positive" that kill the second root of a square — without that word, x^2 = 49 would give 7 or -7 and Statement I alone would fail. The qualifier is doing the work.

**Q19. Answer: (a) Statement I alone is sufficient**
I alone: length 12 and breadth 5 give area = 60 sq cm. Sufficient.
II alone: perimeter 34 means length + breadth = 17, but many rectangles fit (12 and 5, or 10 and 7, giving different areas). Not sufficient.
**Trick:** Perimeter fixes the sum of the sides, not the sides themselves, so it rarely pins down area alone. Confirm sufficiency by checking whether a second valid pair (10 and 7) gives a different answer — it does, so II fails.

**Q20. Answer: (b) Statement II alone is sufficient**
I alone: salary is more than 20,000 — a range, not a value. Not sufficient.
II alone: 40% of 75,000 = 30,000, a single value. Sufficient.
**Trick:** An inequality ("more than", "at least") describes a range and can almost never determine an exact quantity, so cross it off fast. A percentage tied to a known base yields one number — that is your sufficient statement.
