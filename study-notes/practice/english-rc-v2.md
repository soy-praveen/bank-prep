## Answer Key, Solutions & Tricks

**Q1. b)** The passage traces the growth of digital payments while repeatedly warning that older and remote citizens "feel stranded" and must not be "left behind" — the closing line ties progress to "how few it forgets." Option (b) captures both halves. (a), (c), (d) are supporting details, not the main idea; (e) is contradicted, since banks must retain physical currency.
**Trick:** The main idea usually reconciles the two sides of a passage. Options that state only one detail (cheap data, older citizens) are traps; pick the one echoed in the concluding sentence.

**Q2. c)** The passage states directly: "A combination of cheap mobile data, a national digital-payments network and a young population comfortable with screens created fertile ground." The other options (subsidies, more branches, interest rates, currency devaluation) never appear.
**Trick:** For a "which factors" detail question, scan for a listing sentence with "combination of" or commas. The answer is almost verbatim from the text — reject options with unmentioned nouns.

**Q3. c)** The line "financial inclusion... rings hollow if the very people it was meant to help are left behind" implies inclusion is real only when the marginalised are not excluded. (a) and (b) are extremes the author rejects (speed is explicitly downgraded); (d) is never claimed.
**Trick:** Inference answers restate the author's implied condition in neutral words. Avoid absolute options ("every trader stops," "as fast as possible") — the passage argues against speed-for-its-own-sake.

**Q4. b)** "Fertile ground for change" means conditions favourable to change. "Favourable" fits; "barren" is its opposite.
**Trick:** For vocab-in-context, substitute each option into the phrase. "Favourable ground for change" reads naturally; test the antonym (barren) to confirm you haven't inverted the meaning.

**Q5. c)** "Stranded" means left helpless without support; its opposite is "supported." Options (a) abandoned, (b) isolated are near-synonyms — classic distractors.
**Trick:** In antonym questions, the wrong choices are often synonyms of the target word. First cross out anything that means the SAME as "stranded," then pick from what remains.

**Q6. b)** The author praises the shift yet cautions about those left behind ("delicate task," "must widen... without abandoning") — balanced and cautionary. There is no sarcasm, anger, or mourning.
**Trick:** When a passage lists both a benefit and a warning, the tone is "balanced/measured/cautionary." Strongly negative tone words (angry, sarcastic) rarely fit informative business-page prose.

**Q7. b)** The passage centres on residents who "organised weekend clean-ups" and forced policy change, ending with "rivers can be revived, but only when ordinary people refuse to accept their decline." Citizen action driving revival is the theme. (a), (c), (d) are overstated absolutes; (e) is a detail.
**Trick:** The theme often sits in the final sentence's general statement. Reject options containing "never," "always," or "can never" — real passages hedge, and this one shows revival IS possible.

**Q8. c)** Stated directly: "they organised weekend clean-ups, documented every drain that discharged waste, and pressed local officials with data." (d) is wrong — the authorities built the plants after being pressured; (e) contradicts "Rather than wait for the authorities."
**Trick:** Match the sequence of verbs in the text (organised, documented, pressed). The option that lists the SAME actions in the SAME roles is correct; watch for who did what (residents pressured; officials built).

**Q9. c)** "Recovery of any damaged ecosystem is slow and stubborn work" and "did not return... overnight" signal a gradual process needing sustained effort. (a) is explicitly denied; (d) is contradicted by the citizen-led story.
**Trick:** Words like "slow," "stubborn," "not overnight" are inference flags for "gradual." Any option claiming something is "instant" when the text says "not overnight" is an automatic reject.

**Q10. b)** "Persistence eventually forced a change" — persistence here means steadfast determination. "Surrender" and "hesitation" are opposites.
**Trick:** Read the outcome clause: persistence "forced a change," so it must be a positive, forceful quality. Determination fits; passive words (hesitation, surrender) cannot force anything.

**Q11. c)** "Revived" means brought back to life; its direct opposite is "destroyed." Restored, renewed, refreshed and awakened are all synonyms — distractors.
**Trick:** Four synonyms plus one true antonym is a common antonym-question design. Bundle the synonyms together and the odd one out (destroyed) is your answer.

**Q12. b)** Fish return, children watch the water, and the passage insists decline need not be permanent — hopeful and encouraging. It is not "neutral/statistical" because it carries clear optimism and a moral.
**Trick:** If a passage ends on a positive "lesson" or moral, tone is "hopeful/optimistic/inspiring." A "neutral" tag only fits when there is no evaluative language — but here words like "revived" and "refuse to accept" show feeling.

**Q13. b)** The opening contrasts loneliness as "a private sorrow" with the claim it "deserves attention as a public concern," and closes on "a shared problem, not merely a personal failing." Option (b) states this argument. (a) and (d) are contradicted; (c) and (e) are minor points.
**Trick:** Purpose questions on argument passages hinge on the thesis, usually in sentence two ("Recent research, however, suggests..."). The word "however" signals the author's real claim.

**Q14. c)** "Loneliness thrives in silence; those who suffer most are often the least likely to speak of it, fearing they will be judged as weak." The silence-from-fear point is the stated difficulty. (a), (b), (d), (e) are not given as reasons.
**Trick:** Look for the sentence explaining "what makes the issue tricky/difficult." The cause is usually right beside those signposting words — here, the fear of being judged.

**Q15. b)** The author reports critics ("too modest") but gives supporters the reply that "even a small nudge... can interrupt a downward spiral," and calls the shift "important" — showing measured support. (a) overstates; the author does not dismiss the measures.
**Trick:** When an author presents critics AND supporters then adds a positive final judgment ("important shift"), their stance leans toward the supporters. Reject extreme options ("entirely useless," "cannot be reduced at all").

**Q16. b)** "Prolonged social isolation" means extended/lengthy isolation. "Brief" and "sudden" are opposites.
**Trick:** Prefix clue: "pro-longed" literally lengthens. Match to the option meaning long/extended and discard anything meaning short or rare.

**Q17. b)** "Withdraw from work and community life" means to pull back; its opposite is "engage." Retreat, escape, hide and depart are all synonyms of withdraw.
**Trick:** Again spot the four-synonym cluster (retreat/escape/hide/depart) and the lone antonym (engage). The outlier is the answer in an antonym question.

**Q18. b)** The passage weighs studies, costs, and both sides of the debate calmly before drawing a conclusion — thoughtful and analytical. There is no panic ("alarmed/frantic") or mockery.
**Trick:** Research-based passages that cite "studies" and "critics/supporters" are "analytical/reasoned" in tone. Emotional extremes (frantic, hostile, joyful) almost never match evidence-driven prose.

**Q19. b)** "How to prepare a budget" is the standard collocation and fits the list of prudent skills (understanding interest, budgeting, avoiding debt). "Waste" or "ignore" a budget would reverse the intended sense.
**Trick:** Cloze blanks near a positive list take positive verbs. "Prepare/make a budget" is a fixed phrase; reject negative verbs when the sentence praises good habits.

**Q20. b)** The sentence is regretful ("Sadly... leaving young adults to learn the hard way"), so schools "fail to teach these basics." "Manage" or "agree" would clash with "Sadly."
**Trick:** Let the emotion word steer the blank. "Sadly" demands a negative verb; among the choices only "fail" (and arguably refuse) is negative, and "fail to teach" is the natural idiom.

**Q21. a)** "When their first salary arrives, they often spend it without a plan, only to face shortages" — spending without a plan causes month-end shortages. "Save" or "invest" would not lead to shortages.
**Trick:** Read the consequence clause ("only to face shortages"). Work backward: only careless SPENDING produces shortages, so the blank must be "spend."

**Q22. b)** "Setting aside even a modest sum each month grows into a meaningful cushion within a few years." Only "grows" yields a cushion; shrinks/falls/vanishes contradict the positive result.
**Trick:** The phrase "into a meaningful cushion" signals increase. Choose the verb whose direction matches the outcome — savings accumulating means "grows."

**Q23. b)** "Experts also warn against borrowing for things that lose value quickly." "Warn against" is the fixed collocation; welcome/hope/apply don't take "against."
**Trick:** The preposition "against" locks the verb. Test each option with "against": only "warn against" is idiomatic — a fast preposition-collocation shortcut.

**Q24. b)** "The goal is... to feel securely in control of it," contrasted with "not to live in fear of money." "Helplessly" or "barely" in control contradicts the reassuring point.
**Trick:** Contrast markers ("not to live in fear... but to feel ___") demand an opposite-of-fear word. "Securely" opposes fear; helpless/barely reinforce fear, so reject them.

**Q25. b)** "Managing wisely what one already earns" completes the contrast between earning a great deal and managing what you earn. "Owes/borrows/loses" break the sense of having money to manage.
**Trick:** Parallel-idea clue: the sentence pairs "earning a great deal" with managing "what one already ___." The blank mirrors "earning," so "earns" preserves the parallel.
