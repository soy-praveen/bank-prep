### Set A — Subject–Verb Attraction

**Q1 — (b) has.**
(a) **Rule:** The subject is *the list* (singular). The verb agrees with the head noun, not with *candidates* inside the "of" phrase: *the list ... has been published.*
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) have.** People let the nearest plural noun *candidates* pull the verb into the plural.
(c) **Trick:** Mentally delete the "of ___" phrase. "The list ___ been published" — obviously *has*.

**Q2 — (c) has.**
(a) **Rule:** Head noun *quality* is singular → *has improved*. *Answers* and *trainees* are just modifiers.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) have.** The plural *answers* sitting right before the blank tempts the plural verb.
(c) **Trick:** Find the true subject by asking "what improved?" — the *quality*, not the answers.

**Q3 — (d) has been.**
(a) **Rule:** *A bunch* is a singular collective head → *a bunch ... has been lying*. The tense needs perfect-continuous with "since morning," so "has been."
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) are / (c) have been.** *Keys* right before the verb screams plural.
(c) **Trick:** "Since morning" demands a perfect form, and the singular subject fixes it to *has been*.

**Q4 — (b) is.**
(a) **Rule:** *Series* is singular even though it ends in -s and refers to many workshops → *the series ... is scheduled*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) are.** The -s ending and the many workshops both suggest plural.
(c) **Trick:** *Series, species, means, news* look plural but take a singular verb.

**Q5 — (c) were.**
(a) **Rule:** With *neither ... nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject. The nearer one is *assistants* (plural) → *were*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) was.** People default to singular after "neither."
(c) **Trick:** In neither/nor and either/or, the verb obeys whichever subject is closest to it.

### Set B — "The number of" vs "A number of"

**Q6 — (b) has.**
(a) **Rule:** *The number of ___* is singular (it names one figure) → *the number ... has risen*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) have.** *Applicants* just before the verb drags it plural.
(c) **Trick:** "The number" = one number = singular. Always.

**Q7 — (b) have.**
(a) **Rule:** *A number of ___* means "several/many" and is plural → *a number of employees have requested*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) has.** People overcorrect after learning that "the number" is singular and apply it to "a number" too.
(c) **Trick:** THE number → singular; A number → plural. Swap "a number of" with "many" to hear the plural.

**Q8 — (c) is.**
(a) **Rule:** *The number of* → singular → *the number ... is small*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) are.** *Defaulters* pulls it plural.
(c) **Trick:** Same as Q6 — "the number" is one quantity, so *is*.

### Set C — Since vs For

**Q9 — (c) since.**
(a) **Rule:** *Since* marks a *point* in time (a start date). 2015 is a point → *since 2015*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) for.** Learners pair "for" with any duration, forgetting that a fixed year is a point.
(c) **Trick:** SINCE + a point on the calendar/clock; FOR + a length of time.

**Q10 — (b) for.**
(a) **Rule:** *Nearly a decade* is a *duration* → *for nearly a decade*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) since.** The present-perfect "have known" makes people reach for "since" reflexively.
(c) **Trick:** If you can put "a period of" in front of it (a period of nearly a decade), use FOR.

**Q11 — (d) since.**
(a) **Rule:** *Last Monday* is a specific starting point → *since last Monday*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) for.** "Last Monday" feels like recent time, so "for" slips in.
(c) **Trick:** "Last Monday" answers "starting when?" — a point — so SINCE.

### Set D — Senior / Superior / Prefer + TO

**Q12 — (c) senior to.**
(a) **Rule:** Latin-origin comparatives (*senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, anterior*) take *to*, never *than* — and never "more." → *senior to me*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) senior than / (b) more senior than.** English comparison habit ("taller than") makes "than" feel natural.
(c) **Trick:** Words ending in -ior lean on *to*, not *than*.

**Q13 — (b) superior to.**
(a) **Rule:** *Superior* is already comparative and takes *to* → *superior to the earlier one*. "More superior" is a double comparative.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) superior than / (c) more superior to.** Both the "than" reflex and the urge to add "more" strike here.
(c) **Trick:** Never say "more superior." Superior *to*. Done.

**Q14 — (d) prefer.**
(a) **Rule:** *Prefer X to Y*. Here the structure is "prefer tea to coffee," so the verb is simply *prefer*. (Options a and d are identical in wording; the intended correct verb is *prefer*, completing *prefer tea to coffee*.)
(b) **Common wrong answer: (b) rather / (e) prefer than.** People confuse it with "would rather ... than," which is a different pattern.
(c) **Trick:** PREFER pairs with *to*. Only "would rather" pairs with *than*.

**Q15 — (b) prefer / to.**
(a) **Rule:** *Prefer A to B* → *prefer digital statements to paper ones*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) prefer / than.** The comparison "feels" like it needs "than."
(c) **Trick:** Chant it: "prefer TEA to COFFEE" — the slot after prefer's object is always *to*.

### Set E — Less vs Fewer

**Q16 — (c) fewer.**
(a) **Rule:** *Candidates* are countable → *fewer*. (*Less* is for uncountables.)
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) less.** "Less" is the default filler for everyone in speech.
(c) **Trick:** If you can put a number in front (ten candidates), use FEWER.

**Q17 — (b) less.**
(a) **Rule:** *Money* as a mass/uncountable idea → *less money*. (You'd count *rupees*, not *money*.)
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) fewer.** After drilling "fewer," students overcorrect onto uncountables.
(c) **Trick:** Money, water, time, information → uncountable → LESS.

**Q18 — (c) fewer.**
(a) **Rule:** *Items* are countable → *fewer than ten items*. (This is exactly why "10 items or fewer" is the correct sign.)
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) less.** Supermarket "10 items or less" signs have trained the wrong reflex.
(c) **Trick:** Countable plural noun after the blank → FEWER.

### Set F — Correlatives

**Q19 — (b) were / than.**
(a) **Rule:** *No sooner* is followed by *than* (not "when"/"then"). With inversion after a fronted negative you invert subject and auxiliary: *No sooner were the results announced than...*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) had / when or (d) did / than.** People pair "no sooner" with "when," or force "had" where a passive "were" is needed.
(c) **Trick:** NO SOONER ... THAN (they rhyme in the pairing); HARDLY/SCARCELY ... WHEN.

**Q20 — (c) when.**
(a) **Rule:** *Hardly/Scarcely ... when* is the fixed pair → *Hardly had the train left ... when the signal failed*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) than.** "No sooner ... than" bleeds over onto "hardly."
(c) **Trick:** Hardly and Scarcely shake hands with WHEN. Only "no sooner" takes THAN.

**Q21 — (a) not only / but also.**
(a) **Rule:** The matching correlative is *not only ... but also*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (b) not only / but.** Dropping "also" breaks the balanced pair in a formal test context.
(c) **Trick:** Keep correlatives in their fixed sets — "not only" always wants "but also."

**Q22 — (c) Neither / nor.**
(a) **Rule:** *Neither* pairs only with *nor* → *Neither the manager nor the cashier was present*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (b) Neither / or.** "Or" feels interchangeable with "nor."
(c) **Trick:** Neither↔Nor, Either↔Or. Same first letter after the slash: **n**either/**n**or.

### Set G — Redundancy

**Q23 — (c) return.**
(a) **Rule:** *Return* already means "give/send back," so "return back" is redundant → just *return*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) return back / (b) revert back.** Both are everyday spoken redundancies ("revert back" doubles the "back" inside re-).
(c) **Trick:** Any verb with "re-" (return, revert, repeat, reply) rejects a following "back/again."

**Q24 — (b) The two firms decided to cooperate.**
(a) **Rule:** *Cooperate* already contains "with each other/mutually," so those additions are redundant. The clean sentence is (b).
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) or (c).** "Mutually cooperate" and "cooperate with each other" sound emphatic, so they seem richer.
(c) **Trick:** "Co-" and "mutual/each other" are the same idea — keep one.

**Q25 — (c) repeat that point.**
(a) **Rule:** *Repeat* means "say again," so "again" is redundant → *repeat that point*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) repeat that point again.** It's a near-universal spoken habit.
(c) **Trick:** Repeat = again already inside it. Don't stack another "again."

**Q26 — (c) The outcome of the merger is still uncertain.**
(a) **Rule:** *Outcome* and *result* are the same idea; "final outcome," "end result," and "final end result" all double up. Clean version: (c).
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) final outcome / (b) end result.** They sound like standard business English.
(c) **Trick:** An outcome/result is inherently the end thing — drop "final/end."

### Set H — Past Perfect & Sequence of Tenses

**Q27 — (b) had / recovered.**
(a) **Rule:** Of two past events, the *earlier* one takes past perfect. Recovery happened before the arrival → *had already recovered*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) has ... recovered.** Mixing present perfect into a wholly past narration.
(c) **Trick:** "By the time X happened, Y had already happened." The action further back gets HAD.

**Q28 — (a) left / reached.**
(a) **Rule:** With *before/after*, the conjunction already fixes the sequence, so a simple past in both clauses is standard and correct: *The train left before we reached the station.* (Using "had left" is also acceptable, but among the options, the clean simple-past pair is the intended answer; "reached had" combinations in the other options are wrong.)
(b) **Common wrong answer: (d) left / had reached.** Putting "had" on the *later* action (reaching) reverses the real order.
(c) **Trick:** "Before" already shows what came first, so don't force "had" onto the second, later action.

**Q29 — (c) had noticed.**
(a) **Rule:** Reported speech in the past: the earlier noticing shifts to past perfect → *she had noticed ... the previous week*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) has noticed.** Failing to back-shift the tense after "told."
(c) **Trick:** After a past reporting verb (told/said), an earlier action becomes HAD + past participle.

**Q30 — (b) had already left.**
(a) **Rule:** Leaving happened before reaching the counter → past perfect *had already left*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (e) already left / (a) has already left.** Simple past blurs the order; present perfect clashes with the past frame.
(c) **Trick:** Two past actions, one clearly first → first one wears HAD.

### Set I — Article a / an by SOUND

**Q31 — (b) an.**
(a) **Rule:** Choose a/an by the *sound* of the next word. *Hour* starts with a silent H, a vowel sound /aʊ/ → *an hour*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) a.** The written consonant "h" fools the eye.
(c) **Trick:** Say the word aloud. If it opens with a vowel *sound*, use "an" — spelling is irrelevant.

**Q32 — (b) an.**
(a) **Rule:** *MBA* begins with the letter M, pronounced "em" — a vowel sound /e/ → *an MBA*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) a.** "M is a consonant" thinking ignores how the abbreviation is voiced.
(c) **Trick:** For abbreviations, use the letter's spoken name: M = "em," so "an."

**Q33 — (b) a.**
(a) **Rule:** *European* starts with a "y" consonant sound /juː/ → *a European*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) an.** The written vowel "E" invites "an."
(c) **Trick:** "Eu-" and "Uni-" words sound like "you-" — a consonant sound — so use "a" (a European, a university, a useful tool).

**Q34 — (b) an.**
(a) **Rule:** *Honest* has a silent H → vowel sound /ɒ/ → *an honest mistake*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) a.** The consonant letter "h" again.
(c) **Trick:** The silent-H club — honest, hour, honour, heir — all take "an."

**Q35 — (b) a.**
(a) **Rule:** *University* opens with a "yoo" consonant sound → *a university degree*.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) an.** The letter "u" looks like a vowel.
(c) **Trick:** If the word sounds like it starts with "y," it takes "a." (a university, a European, a one-time offer).

### Set J — Para-jumble Openers & the Pronoun-Hug

**Q36 — (b) Q.**
(a) **Rule:** The opener must introduce the subject with a *full noun*, not a pronoun. Only (Q) — "a small startup launched a budgeting tool" — names things fresh. P's *It*, R's *They/it*, and S's *Its* all point back to nouns in Q, so they can't come first.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) P.** "It quickly became the most downloaded app" reads like a punchy headline opener, but "It" has no antecedent yet.
(c) **Trick:** A sentence starting with a pronoun (It/They/This/Its) can never open a paragraph — it needs an earlier noun to hug.

**Q37 — (b) 1 then 2.**
(a) **Rule:** *They* in (2) refers to *the new guidelines* in (1), so (1) must come first and (2) must follow immediately — the pronoun hugs its noun.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) 2 then 1.** Testing whether you'll place the pronoun before its antecedent.
(c) **Trick:** Noun first, pronoun right behind it — never the reverse.

**Q38 — (c) This has delighted the students of the area.**
(a) **Rule:** *This* is a demonstrative pronoun with no antecedent yet, so (c) cannot open. (Also, (d) "had spent" is a past-perfect follow-up that needs an earlier past event to relate to, but the clearest impossible opener is the pronoun-led (c).)
(b) **Common wrong answer: (d) Volunteers had spent months...** Its past perfect feels mid-story, but it at least names a full subject; the demonstrative "This" in (c) is the definitive non-opener.
(c) **Trick:** Opening lines can't lead with This/That/These/Those/It/They — they all need a prior noun.

### Set K — Contrast Connectors

**Q39 — (d) Despite.**
(a) **Rule:** *Despite / In spite of* are followed by a *noun/noun phrase*. "The heavy rain" is a noun phrase → *Despite the heavy rain*. (Although/Though/While would need a full clause with a verb.)
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) Although.** People treat all contrast words as interchangeable.
(c) **Trick:** DESPITE + noun (no verb); ALTHOUGH + subject + verb. There's no verb after the blank here, so it must be *Despite*.

**Q40 — (c) Although.**
(a) **Rule:** "___ she had prepared thoroughly" is a full clause (subject *she* + verb *had prepared*) → it needs *Although*. *Despite/In spite of/Regardless of* would require a noun, not a clause.
(b) **Common wrong answer: (a) Despite / (b) In spite of.** Both express contrast, so they tempt you, but they can't sit before a clause without "the fact that."
(c) **Trick:** See a subject + verb after the blank? Use ALTHOUGH/THOUGH. See only a noun? Use DESPITE/IN SPITE OF. (*Owing to* means "because of," the wrong meaning entirely.)
